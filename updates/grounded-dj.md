# Safer, more natural DJ links

Good radio links sound as though somebody is listening to the record—not as
though somebody is reading a database. But a DJ should not have to choose
between sounding natural and staying accurate.

SUB/WAVE now gives the DJ a small, approved set of facts for each link. The DJ
can react, make a connection and speak in their own voice, but music-history
claims stay within what the station actually knows. Private track-selection
notes and other behind-the-scenes context do not become on-air lore.

## What listeners will notice

The change is mostly one of trust and texture. Links can still be warm,
opinionated, playful or understated, depending on the presenter and your
station. A DJ can say how a record lands, set up the next moment, or make a
natural acknowledgement as one show gives way to another.

What should disappear are the confident-sounding leaps: an invented release
date, an assumed credit, a claim about an artist's intentions, or a bit of
selection logic presented as if the listener ought to know it. When SUB/WAVE
has a useful fact, it can use it. When it does not, the DJ has room to be a
presenter rather than a guesser.

## The difference in a link

The following is illustrative, rather than a transcript from a particular
station. Assume SUB/WAVE knows only that **Mira Vale — “Glass Lines”** was
released in **2018**.

**Before: operational language leaking into the link**

> “The atmosphere is shifting gently here—Mira Vale’s *Glass Lines* keeps that
> reflective energy moving us on through another musical journey.”

**Now: a presenter responding to the record**

> “Mira Vale with *Glass Lines*. There’s a lovely glow to this one—released in
> 2018, and settling in beautifully.”

The second link still has a point of view. “Lovely glow” is the DJ’s
in-character reaction, not a claim that needs external proof. But it does not
turn private selection terms into radio copy or invent a story around the
record.

## A factual boundary, not a script

For a track link, SUB/WAVE passes the DJ only a compact set of verified
information. It always identifies the track and artist when they are known,
and may add a small number of relevant details such as its album, a trusted
release year, or a carefully bounded piece of station history.

Those details are optional material, not a checklist. A DJ does not need to
recite every available fact, and a good link will often use none beyond the
record itself. The boundary is there to stop an uncertain detail being filled
in from general model knowledge—not to make every break sound informational.

The same principle protects the line around a show change. The DJ can mention
an approaching handover and introduce the following show when it fits, without
turning it into a countdown or repeatedly narrating the station clock.

## What this does not do

This is not a music-trivia feature or a guarantee that every link will contain
extra detail. It does not research the web, turn every break into a fact dump,
or make a fuller story available for every record.

The optional, source-backed **Extended Sleeve Notes** feature remains separate
from this update. It is intended to add richer editorial context later; the
factual boundary described here works without it.

Good operator input still matters. A clear DJ brief and well-considered house
rules give the presenter a useful point of view; factual grounding ensures that
point of view does not become invented information.

## What you can control

<!-- Screenshot placeholder: Settings → DJ behaviour → Link style, showing the
Regular, Occasional and Rare release-year mention controls. -->

In **Settings → DJ behaviour → Link style**, choose how often release years are
made available for eligible links:

- **Regular** keeps them available whenever SUB/WAVE has a trusted year.
- **Occasional** makes them available for roughly one in four eligible links.
- **Rare** makes them available for roughly one in six eligible links.

This is a control for variety, not for truth. The library's verified year does
not change; the setting simply prevents a useful detail becoming a repeated
habit. The choice is made consistently for each link, so a retry does not
produce a different editorial decision.

Your presenter, house rules and show brief still matter. They shape the
personality, restraint and point of view of a link. They do not override the
factual boundary, which means a vivid persona can remain vivid without being
asked to improvise a history lesson.

## A final safeguard for speech

SUB/WAVE also checks generated speech before it reaches the voice engine.
That final pass removes malformed or production-style bracketed directions
that do not belong on air, while preserving short, supported delivery cues
when they are placed naturally with spoken words. It is a safety net, not a
replacement for good prompting or a well-shaped DJ brief.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does this make the DJ less creative?

No. It separates factual claims from creative response. The DJ can still have
a point of view, describe their reaction to the music, and sound like the
presenter you designed. They simply do not invent externally checkable detail
to make a link feel fuller.

### Will every link mention the album or release year?

No. Verified details are offered sparingly, and the DJ may leave them unused.
The Link style setting lets you further reduce the availability of release
years when you want a lighter metadata touch.

### What information is kept out of the link?

Track-ranking and selection context, operational prompts, and other
behind-the-scenes planning material stay out of the DJ's on-air fact packet.
They can help SUB/WAVE run the station, but they are not listener-facing
claims.

### Is a fact packet a guarantee that every sentence will be factual?

It is a deliberately strong boundary, not a claim that generative writing is
infallible. The DJ is instructed to treat supplied music facts as its only
source for factual claims, and SUB/WAVE adds output checks before speech is
played. As an operator, you should still give your DJ clear house rules and a
brief that suits your station.
