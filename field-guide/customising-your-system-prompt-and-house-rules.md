# Customising your system prompt and station house rules

Most stations should begin with SUB/WAVE’s built-in system prompt. It already
sets the shared baseline for natural, concise, fact-aware radio speech, leaving
your DJs, shows and settings to do the interesting station-specific work.

SUB/WAVE does **not** ship with default station house rules. That field starts
empty; it is there only when your station has a small set of additional rules
that genuinely must apply everywhere.

When you do need to customise it, make the smallest change that solves a real
station-wide problem. The system prompt and station house rules are powerful;
they are also shared instructions, so a vague or overlong edit can flatten
every presenter at once.

That does not mean every good custom prompt is short. A fuller shared contract
can earn its place after careful listening and testing. The test is whether
each part protects a real on-air outcome—not whether the prompt simply sounds
impressive or comprehensive.

## Two tools, different jobs

| Use this | When you need to change |
| --- | --- |
| **System prompt** | The common writing frame for scripted DJ talk across all personas: how a DJ is introduced, broad delivery guidance, and the baseline rules around generated speech. |
| **Station house rules** | A non-negotiable rule that must apply to every spoken output, including scripted talk, agent-written request speech and multi-presenter banter. |

A **Soul** remains the place for one DJ’s personality. A **show brief** defines
one programme. **Musical Leanings** guide a close music choice. Do not move
those local instructions into a station-wide prompt just because the prompt is
more prominent.

## Start with the built-in default

The built-in prompt is a working radio baseline, not an empty text box. It
keeps spoken output plain, short and varied; discourages radio-cliché phrasing
and robotic repetition; and tells DJs to use supplied context naturally rather
than inventing facts around the station.

Leave the House rules field empty unless you have a real station-wide
requirement to add. An empty field is the normal default, not an incomplete
setup.

If you want a different default feel, create a copy and change one clear
dimension. For example, a station may want a more formal public-radio register,
a more playful community-radio register, or a different standard form of
English. Keep the DJ placeholders intact—especially the presenter name and
Soul—so individual voices still have somewhere to live.

## Edit for an outcome you can hear

Good prompt changes describe a listener-facing result:

- “Use British spelling and phrasing.”
- “Keep station IDs direct and unshowy.”
- “Favour plain, conversational language over promotional copy.”

Weak prompt changes are often large lists of moods, banned words, metaphors and
performer instructions. They make every DJ solve the same long negative brief
before it can speak, and can pull a varied roster towards one cautious voice.

Before changing the prompt, ask whether the issue belongs somewhere smaller:
a Soul for one presenter, a show brief for one programme, or a Musical Leaning
for a close music choice. If it does, leave the shared prompt alone.

## A tested station example

Four Acres FM developed a fuller custom prompt and House Rules block after
several weeks of observation and live testing. Its shared prompt establishes a
clear on-air boundary: the DJ speaks as a presenter, keeps production material
backstage, treats supplied material as the factual boundary, and does not force
a fact, anecdote, joke or musical observation into every link.

Its House Rules then carry the station-wide requirements that must survive
every writing path: a friendly personal-station relationship with listeners,
the same factual boundary, and specific rules for local references and
performance cues. This is a good use of the two layers: the prompt provides a
complete writing frame where it runs, while the House Rules reinforce the
invariants that must also reach requests and multi-presenter speech.

Do not copy that arrangement blindly. In particular, performance-cue wording
must match your voice engine, and a station's location, listener relationship
and factual needs are its own. Borrow the method: observe a repeated failure,
write the narrowest clear rule that addresses it, then listen again.

## Use house rules for true station invariants

House rules are the right home for requirements you never want a DJ to skip,
regardless of which writing path produced the speech. Typical examples include:

- spell out numbers, dates and units in words for speech;
- use a chosen spelling convention;
- follow a station-wide pronunciation or TTS-delivery convention; and
- preserve a short, explicit safety or factual rule that applies to all spoken
  material.

Keep each rule concrete and testable. “Spell out dates in words” is a house
rule. “Be warm, fascinating and never repetitive” is not: it is vague, belongs
to different presenters and programmes in different ways, and cannot reliably
be checked.

House rules are deliberately broader than the system-prompt template. They are
added to scripted DJ talk, tool-using paths and guest exchanges alike. That is
why they should be stable and genuinely universal. A detailed factual or
delivery rule can be worthwhile; a collection of individual presenter tastes
is not.

## Bob does not need a custom prompt

Bob is a good test. His guitar-music taste, dry warmth and interest in musical
craft do not justify changing the station’s common prompt. They belong in his
Soul, his show’s settings and brief, and his Musical Leanings.

Putting “talk about great guitar music” or a list of Bob’s favourite artists
in the system prompt would make every DJ inherit Bob’s taste. It would also
turn a useful shared baseline into a second, competing show brief.

The same test works for any proposed edit: if it would sound strange coming
from a different host on a different programme, it is not a station-wide
prompt rule.

## Change one thing and listen

Save a named prompt template rather than overwriting your only working setup.
Then listen across several kinds of output: an ordinary track link, a station
ID, a request, a scheduled segment and, if you use them, guest exchanges.

Look for the actual outcome you intended—and for unintended sameness. If every
DJ starts using the same opening, metaphor or level of formality, shorten the
shared instruction and move personality back to the Soul.

## A quick check before saving

1. Is this a requirement for every DJ and every show?
2. Can a listener hear whether it is working?
3. Is the instruction clear, specific and no longer than it needs to be?
4. Have I kept the built-in factual and natural-speech protections intact?
5. Have I saved a version I can return to if the change does not work?

If the answer is yes, you are using the shared layer for what it is best at:
giving the whole station a dependable foundation while leaving its people and
programmes room to differ.
