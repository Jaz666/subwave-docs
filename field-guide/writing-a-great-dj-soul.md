# Writing a great DJ Soul

A DJ Soul is a short description of the person listeners hear. It gives the DJ
a voice, a way of noticing the world, and a relationship with the audience. It
should make a presenter recognisable across different shows without trying to
choose the music, write every link, or contain a complete life story.

Think of it as casting, not programming.

## What belongs in a Soul

The most useful Souls usually have four small ingredients:

- **Voice:** warm, dry, forthright, gentle, mischievous, precise, excitable.
- **Attention:** the details this person tends to notice—a great bassline, an
  odd production choice, the feeling of a room, a surprising connection.
- **Relationship:** how they treat listeners—companionable, curious, teasing,
  reassuring, understated.
- **Restraint:** what they do not become—never a lecturer, never a hype person,
  never a comedian on every link.

Those are enough to create a point of view. The rest belongs elsewhere.

| Keep it out of the Soul | Put it here instead |
| --- | --- |
| Genres, eras, energy and exclusions | **Show settings** |
| Named artists that must appear | **Show playlist** |
| A preference that settles close music choices | **Musical Leanings** |
| The purpose and pace of one programme | **Show brief** |
| Station-wide non-negotiables | **Station house rules** |

## Meet Bob: the common first draft

Bob's original Soul is the kind of generous first draft a new user may receive
from a writing assistant. It gives him a musical history, lists favourite
artists and genres, describes production preferences, and explains what he
does not like. All of that establishes a clear taste—but it is doing too many
jobs at once.

Here is an abridged version:

> “Bob is a lifelong music obsessive with an encyclopaedic knowledge of rock,
> indie and alternative music. He loves classic rock, alternative rock, indie,
> Britpop, post-punk and progressive rock, with favourites including Pink Floyd,
> R.E.M., The Cure, The Smiths, Radiohead, Oasis, Blur and The Stone Roses. He
> loves melodic guitar music, prominent basslines, acoustic guitars,
> interesting production and strong musicianship. He prefers deeper album
> tracks and overlooked gems over obvious hits. Warm, knowledgeable and
> enthusiastic, he loves sharing musical discoveries with listeners.”

The useful human core is already there: Bob is warm, knowledgeable,
enthusiastic, attentive to musical craft, and enjoys helping a listener notice
something worthwhile. The artist list, genre boundaries and deep-cut preference
do not need to live in the Soul.

## A better Soul for Bob

> “Bob is warm, dryly funny and genuinely delighted by a record that rewards a
> closer listen. He notices the small things—a bass part holding a song
> together, a guitar sound that changes the room, an arrangement that takes an
> unexpected turn—but shares them lightly, never like a lecture. He treats
> listeners as fellow music fans rather than an audience to impress, and would
> rather offer one good observation than fill every silence.”

This version gives Bob a voice and a habit of attention. It can work on an
afternoon alternative show, as a guest on a broader programme, or in a link
where the available facts are sparse. His musical territory and close-call
preferences can change with the show without making Bob sound like a different
person.

## Avoid the biography trap

An elaborate backstory can be fun, especially for a fictional station. But
every extra fact is another thing the DJ may try to repeat, explain or turn
into a performance. The best test is simple: will this detail change how the
presenter speaks or notices a record?

Keep the details that do. Leave out the rest unless a show brief or a specific
feature genuinely needs it.

For example, “spent years collecting strange second-hand records” may explain
why a DJ loves a good find. A list of ten favourite bands does not reliably
make those bands available for selection, and often contributes less to the
voice than one sentence about how the DJ shares discoveries.

## Give the DJ room to vary

A Soul should create recognisable behaviour, not a catchphrase generator.
Avoid instructions such as “always mention the guitar tone,” “make a joke in
every link,” or “talk about the artist's history.” They make an appealing trait
mechanical very quickly.

Prefer tendencies:

- “Has an ear for the detail that holds a record together.”
- “Enjoys a dry observation, but never forces a joke.”
- “Makes the listener feel they have been let in on a good find.”
- “Keeps the language plain even when the music is ambitious.”

These leave the DJ free to read the moment, use supplied facts only when they
help, and stay quiet when the music needs the space.

## Write Souls for a shared station

Distinct Souls make a station feel like it has a real roster. Avoid giving
every DJ the same “warm, knowledgeable music obsessive” shape. Change the
angle: one host may be quietly observant, another bright and quick-witted,
another patient with strange music, another a natural guide for new releases.

They do not need to disagree or become exaggerated caricatures. Small
differences in attention, pace and humour are more durable than a wardrobe of
catchphrases.

## A quick check before saving

1. Does this describe a person listeners could recognise after several shows?
2. Have I moved music rules, artist requests and close-call taste to their
   proper settings?
3. Does it include one or two natural restraints?
4. Could this DJ speak naturally when there is no special fact to mention?
5. Is it short enough that its strongest idea will not get lost?

If it passes those checks, the Soul gives the DJ a character without taking
away the show’s flexibility.
