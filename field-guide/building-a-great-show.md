# Building a great show

A great show is more than a selection of music with a presenter attached. It
has a reason to exist at that hour, a musical territory listeners can learn,
and enough freedom to keep revealing something new after the first week.

In SUB/WAVE, that identity comes from several small decisions working together:
the structured music settings, the show brief, the host and any guests, the
playlists you deliberately use, and the degree of discovery you leave open.
None has to do every job.

## Start with a clear promise

Before choosing genres or writing a word of prompt text, finish this sentence:

> “This show is for listeners who want…”

The answer may be quiet companionship in the small hours, a gradual lift into
the working day, a warm drive home, a deep dive into unfamiliar sounds, or a
shared enthusiasm for a particular musical world. The promise should describe
the experience of listening, not just the catalogue.

“Two hours of indie and rock” is a useful filter starting point. “A relaxed
afternoon for people who want familiar guitar music with rewarding turns off
the obvious path” is a programme. The first tells SUB/WAVE where to look; the
second tells the host what kind of company to keep.

## Build the musical territory in layers

Use structured show settings for the boundaries that must be applied
consistently: genres, eras, moods, energy, playlists, exclusions and strict
filters. Then decide which of those are genuine rules and which are an
editorial centre of gravity.

A focused show can use a narrow playlist or strict filter when its promise
depends on it. A specialist late-night electronic programme, for example, may
need firm limits on energy so an abrupt change does not break the atmosphere.
An afternoon discovery show may instead use broader settings and let its brief
and presenter guide the balance between familiar favourites and surprise.

The question is not “strict or soft?” in the abstract. Ask what would genuinely
break the programme if it appeared, and make only that a hard boundary.

## Give discovery a job

Discovery is most satisfying when it has a purpose. A new record can refresh a
well-known sound; a deep cut can change how a familiar artist is heard; an odd
cover can make the link between two otherwise distant records feel worthwhile.

Leave some room for that purpose in every show that wants to remain alive. The
default Track Shortlist already makes a place for Context, Continuity and
Exploration. Your show settings decide what is allowed into that field; the
DJ's Musical Leanings can help settle close calls inside it.

If every source is a tightly locked playlist, the station may become reliable
but brittle. If nothing has a centre, it may become varied but anonymous. Good
programming lives between those extremes.

## Let the brief describe the listener's experience

The show brief is where the programme becomes human. Give it the listener's
moment, the desired pace, the host's role, and the restraint that protects the
show from its own bad habits.

Your schedule can contain very different examples of this shape: a breakfast
programme that treats its early and later hours as one continuous conversation;
a late-night show that speaks sparingly and offers company rather than advice;
or a curiosity-led programme that allows an odd record to remain unexplained.
Those are all strong promises because they tell the DJ what the listener should
feel, without asking it to narrate every track or repeat the same slogan.

For practical brief-writing patterns, see [Writing a great DJ
brief](writing-a-great-dj-brief.md).

## Make hosts and guests distinct

A guest should alter the programme, not simply create more speech. Give each
person a different way of noticing music:

- one presenter might bring calm continuity and a feel for the listener's
  moment;
- another might notice musicianship, recording choices or live performance;
- a third might bring newer discoveries, scenes or unexpected connections.

They do not need to perform a scene in every break. A brief “as friends sharing
records” is often enough, especially when it allows occasional warmth,
disagreement and silence rather than demanding them on cue.

## Use Bob to test a show idea

Bob's first draft wants an alternative afternoon of classic favourites, deeper
cuts, guitar texture and new discoveries. That is a promising show, but it
becomes more useful when split into its parts:

| Element | A better home |
| --- | --- |
| Alternative, indie, Britpop and post-punk territory | Genre, era, energy and playlist settings |
| Named artists that must genuinely appear | A real show playlist, not a name in prose |
| Preference for album tracks, guitar texture and rewarding left turns | Bob's Musical Leanings |
| Warm, wry afternoon company with one telling musical detail | Bob's Soul and show brief |

That leaves the show with a recognisable identity but not a fixed sequence. It
can honour its musical world, surprise its listeners and still sound like Bob.

## Review the show after a week, not a single hour

The first hour of a new show is often flattering. The real question is whether
it still has shape after repeated listening. Review a few days of choices and
links together:

- Are the same artists, moods or images appearing too often?
- Does discovery feel like a reward, or like a random interruption?
- Are the hosts distinct without becoming caricatures?
- Does the show stay true to its promise at different points in the day?
- Is any hard rule stopping moments you actually want to hear?

Change one layer at a time. Tighten a filter, revise a Leaning, adjust the
brief, or alter a playlist—but resist rewriting everything after one awkward
transition. A show becomes trustworthy through steady listening and small,
intentional corrections.

## A quick show-building checklist

1. Write the listener promise in one sentence.
2. Set only the musical boundaries that must be consistent.
3. Decide what discovery is allowed to contribute.
4. Give the host and guests distinct, modest roles.
5. Write a brief about the experience, not a list of artists.
6. Listen across several days before making the next adjustment.

That is enough structure for a show to feel designed, and enough openness for
it to keep becoming itself.
