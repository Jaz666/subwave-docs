# Giving your DJ Musical Leanings

Musical Leanings give a DJ a little private musical judgement without asking a
speaking persona to run the station. They are a short description of the kinds
of *eligible* records a presenter tends to favour when there is a genuine close
call.

They are deliberately separate from a DJ's Soul. A Soul tells SUB/WAVE how a
presenter sounds and relates to listeners. Musical Leanings help the DJ choose
between several records that already fit the show, your library rules, and the
current musical moment.

## Meet Bob

Bob is a deliberately familiar example: a warm, knowledgeable guitar-music
DJ, built in the kind of rich prose new SUB/WAVE users often receive from a
general-purpose writing assistant. He loves the great alternative, indie,
Britpop, post-punk and classic-rock artists; he values melodic guitars, strong
basslines, deeper cuts and overlooked discoveries.

There is nothing wrong with that starting point. The useful next step is
separating Bob's **voice**, his **show's territory**, and his **musical
instinct** so that each part does one clear job.

Bob's original Soul, shortened to the parts that matter here, reads:

> “Bob is a lifelong music obsessive with an encyclopaedic knowledge of rock,
> indie and alternative music. He loves classic rock, alternative rock, indie,
> Britpop, post-punk and progressive rock, with favourites including Pink Floyd,
> R.E.M., The Cure, The Smiths, Radiohead, Oasis, Blur and The Stone Roses.
> He loves melodic guitar music, prominent basslines, acoustic guitars,
> interesting production and strong musicianship. He prefers deeper album
> tracks and overlooked gems over obvious hits, and enjoys newer artists
> influenced by the music he grew up with. Warm, knowledgeable and
> enthusiastic, he loves sharing musical discoveries with listeners.”

## Naming artists is not a selection strategy

Bob's first draft names favourite artists such as R.E.M., The Cure, The Smiths,
Radiohead, Oasis, Blur and The Stone Roses. It is an understandable way to
describe a taste, but a list of artists in a Soul or show brief is not a
reliable request for SUB/WAVE to find, rotate or prioritise those artists.

The shortlist is built from the eligible music in your own library. If a named
artist is not present in the library, not surfaced by the available discovery
sources, excluded by the show, or ruled out by recency and variety safeguards,
putting their name in prose does not make them a candidate. A long favourites
list can also make a DJ's taste sound narrower than the station you actually
want to hear.

Give the show its musical territory with its structured genre, era, mood,
energy, playlist and filter controls. Then use Musical Leanings to describe
the *qualities* that help Bob choose between already-eligible records.

For Bob, that might be:

> “Favour melodic, guitar-led records with a strong rhythm section, memorable
> songwriting and some textural detail. When the choice is close, prefer an
> overlooked album track or a newer discovery that feels connected to the
> show’s alternative and post-punk roots over the obvious familiar hit.”

That gives the DJ a real preference—depth over familiarity, musical texture
over a named-artist checklist—while leaving the show and station safeguards in
charge.

## Where Bob's first draft belongs

Bob's original description contains useful material. It simply becomes more
effective when each part goes to the setting designed for it.

| Part of Bob's draft | Best home | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Warm, knowledgeable and enthusiastic” | **Soul** | This shapes how Bob speaks, reacts and relates to listeners. |
| Alternative, indie, Britpop and post-punk | **Show settings** | Genres, eras, energy and other show controls establish the programme's musical territory. |
| “Avoid manufactured pop” and “keep enough energy for the afternoon” | **Show settings and filters** | These are station or programme boundaries, not a close-call preference. |
| Deep cuts, overlooked gems, melodic guitars and strong rhythm sections | **Musical Leanings** | These help choose between several records that already fit. |
| Favourite artists | **Show playlist, when genuinely required** | A playlist provides real library candidates; prose names alone do not. |

## If Bob really means named artists

Sometimes a show genuinely should draw from particular artists. In that case,
put their records in a dedicated show playlist and choose the appropriate
playlist and filter settings for the programme. That gives SUB/WAVE a real,
eligible source of music rather than hoping a name in a paragraph will be
interpreted as a selection command.

Use this deliberately. A playlist is for defining a real musical pool; Musical
Leanings are for giving the DJ taste within or alongside that pool. Bob can
still discover a new record with the right guitar texture or writing instinct
without every afternoon becoming a roll call of the same favourites.

## What a good Leaning does

A useful Leaning is specific enough to break a tie, but modest enough to leave
the show in charge. It describes a musical instinct, not a list of demands.

Good examples include:

- “Favour overlooked album tracks over the obvious single when both fit.”
- “Enjoy gentle changes in energy; save sharp turns for when the programme asks
  for one.”
- “Look for unexpected connections across eras, especially when the rhythm or
  guitar texture makes the link feel natural.”
- “When the field is close, lean towards warm basslines, odd percussion and
  records with a little room to breathe.”

These give the DJ an editorial preference, not an instruction to ignore the
rest of the station.

## A real close call: Lucy at 05:15

On one early-morning pick, **Lucy** followed **The Orb — “dragon of the ocean
(dogon mix)”**. Her Musical Leanings favour broad melodic music, warm voices,
thoughtful songwriting, gradual reveals, deeper catalogue choices and new
discoveries.

The controller had already applied the show's rules and built a shortlist from
three sources:

| Discovery source | Candidate tracks returned |
| --- | ---: |
| Morning, low-energy mood match | 8 |
| Sound-alike matches for the current Orb track | 8 |
| Starred songs | 8 |

Lucy selected **Morcheeba — “Blue Chair”**, which came from the morning,
low-energy group. It was one of several eligible choices, but it aligned with
the presenter's preference for a calm, melodic record that opens up gradually.

The private Booth Log recorded the selection this way:

> “Morcheeba and Blue Chair fit the calm morning atmosphere with their soothing
> melodies, gentle pace, and low energy. Their reflective mood matches Lucy’s
> broad melodic tastes spanning indie, alternative pop, singer-songwriters,
> classic pop, and folk-influenced music.”

This reasoning is for the Booth, not the listener. It shows the DJ making an
editorial connection between the eligible choice, the moment, and Lucy’s
Leanings; it is not a script for the on-air link.

The Leaning did not make “Blue Chair” eligible, command the DJ to choose it,
or override the show. It simply gave the final decision a little more identity
once the station had established a safe field of options.

If “Blue Chair” had broken a strict filter, repeated an artist too soon, or
made the transition feel wrong, the Leaning would have no authority to rescue
it. That is exactly the point.

## Keep Soul and Leanings separate

It is tempting to put musical taste inside a rich DJ Soul. Usually, resist
that. A Soul may say that a presenter is dry, curious, funny, tender or deeply
knowledgeable; those qualities help the DJ speak naturally, but are unreliable
instructions for picking music.

Musical Leanings are private selection guidance. They are never intended for
the DJ to repeat on air, and they do not turn into an explanation of why a
track was selected. Keeping the two fields separate makes both more useful:
the Soul remains a description of a person, while the Leaning remains a small
piece of editorial taste.

## How much to write

Start with one or two sentences. Describe patterns, not individual records or
an exhaustive genre list. The goal is to guide a close decision without making
every selection feel predetermined.

Too vague:

> “Has great taste and plays good music.”

Too controlling:

> “Always choose 1980s post-punk, never play anything slow, and always pick a
> deep cut.”

More useful:

> “Enjoys melodic post-punk, deep soul cuts and surprising cover versions.
> Favour the less obvious record when it keeps the programme moving naturally.”

The last version gives the DJ a recognisable instinct, while still leaving room
for the show, station safeguards and the music already on air.

## Guests and shared shows

A guest can have Musical Leanings too. They can add a light, temporary nudge
to a close decision, helping a guest appearance feel distinct without taking
control away from the regular host or the show's musical brief.

The host's Leanings remain stronger. This prevents a guest's preferences from
overriding the identity and obligations of the programme they have joined.

## A quick check before saving

Ask three questions:

1. Would this help choose between two already-suitable records?
2. Does it describe a tendency rather than a command?
3. Would the station still sound right if the DJ followed it only occasionally?

If the answer is yes, it is probably doing the right amount of work.
