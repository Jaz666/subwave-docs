# Writing a great DJ brief

In this guide, a **DJ brief** means the brief for a particular show. It tells a
presenter what kind of programme they are making *today*: who it is for, what
the moment feels like, how the conversation should move, and what restraint
looks like.

It is not a substitute for a DJ's Soul, a playlist, or your show’s structured
music settings. A strong brief gives a DJ a programme to inhabit without trying
to script every thought, name every record, or explain every musical choice.

## Give each part one job

The clearest shows divide their instructions between the settings that can
enforce them and the prose that can bring them to life.

| Put it here | For this kind of decision |
| --- | --- |
| **DJ Soul** | Who this person is on air: their voice, humour, curiosity and relationship with listeners. |
| **Show settings** | The musical territory: genres, eras, moods, energy, playlists, exclusions and strict filters. |
| **Musical Leanings** | A presenter's private taste when several eligible records are close calls. |
| **Show brief** | The programme's purpose, audience, pace, conversation, relationships and sense of occasion. |

This division is liberating. The brief does not have to carry a complete
biography, every favourite artist and every music rule. It can concentrate on
the thing a listener actually experiences: a distinct programme with a reason
to exist.

## Start with the listener's moment

The most useful briefs describe where the listener is meeting the programme.
Morning shows can be warm and gradual rather than sounding as though every link
is a new breakfast launch. A drive-home show may feel companionable after work.
Late-night radio can offer quiet company without becoming sombre or sleepy.

That is more useful than a generic instruction to “be upbeat” or “play great
music.” It gives the DJ a relationship to the hour and helps it decide when to
talk, when to leave space, and what kind of observation belongs.

On a well-shaped schedule, one morning programme can be explicitly the opening
chapter of a longer breakfast conversation, while the following show picks up
the same thread and welcomes new listeners without pretending the station has
just begun. A late-night show can be equally specific in the opposite
direction: occasional, reassuring links and generous room for the music.

## Write the programme, not a playlist

New show briefs often begin with a long list of genres and favourite artists.
Those are useful clues about taste, but they do not make a compelling
programme—and a name in prose is not a reliable way to make an artist appear
in a shortlist.

Put the musical territory into structured show settings and, where an artist
or group of records is genuinely required, use a real show playlist. Then use
the brief to explain what happens *around* the music.

Instead of:

> “Play lots of classic alternative and indie from the 80s and 90s, especially
> the usual favourite artists, with deeper cuts and new discoveries.”

Try:

> “A relaxed afternoon for listeners who want familiar guitar music with a few
> rewarding turns off the obvious path. The host notices the craft in a record
> without turning each link into a lesson: a telling detail, a dry observation,
> or a gentle connection is enough. Let well-known favourites sit beside album
> tracks and newer discoveries, and leave room for the music to make its case.”

The settings establish what is eligible. The brief establishes why the show
feels different from a random run of eligible tracks.

## Meet Bob: improving a common first draft

Bob is our deliberately familiar new-user example: an enthusiastic,
knowledgeable guitar-music DJ whose first Soul and show brief mention a long
list of favourite artists, deeper cuts, strong musicianship, and what he does
not want to play.

His original show brief reads:

> “Bob's Alternative Afternoon is packed with great guitar music from the 80s,
> 90s and 2000s, mixing alternative rock, indie, Britpop and classic rock. Play
> plenty of Bob's favourite artists such as R.E.M., The Cure, The Smiths,
> Radiohead, Oasis, Blur and The Stone Roses, alongside similar artists and
> newer discoveries. Bob loves melodic guitars, acoustic tracks, strong
> basslines and interesting production, so favour songs with those qualities.
> Avoid manufactured pop, repetitive dance music and novelty records. Don't
> just play the obvious hits — dig deeper into albums and find forgotten
> favourites, overlooked gems and tracks listeners might not have heard for
> years. Keep the music varied but recognisably Bob, with a thoughtful,
> knowledgeable feel and enough energy for the afternoon.”

That is a good description of a taste, but it bundles together four different
jobs. The practical rewrite is:

1. Put genres, eras, energy and any real artist pool in **show settings** and
   playlists.
2. Keep Bob's warmth, enthusiasm and eye for a telling musical detail in his
   **Soul**.
3. Put his preference for album tracks, guitar texture and unexpected
   discoveries in **Musical Leanings**.
4. Give the show brief a listener, a time of day and a conversational purpose.

Bob's rewritten afternoon brief might be:

> “Bob keeps thoughtful company through the afternoon with guitar-led music
> that rewards a closer listen. He is warm, wry and genuinely pleased to share
> a discovery, but never sounds as though he is delivering a lecture. Let a
> familiar favourite lead naturally into an overlooked album track or a newer
> connection; one memorable detail is more useful than a list of facts. Keep
> the pace easy and the conversation unforced, with enough space for the
> records to speak for themselves.”

This does not select the music by itself. It tells Bob how to turn a valid
musical field into an afternoon listeners recognise.

## Give co-hosts a reason to be there

When more than one presenter appears, the brief should give them distinct
angles—not a script, and not a row of interchangeable voices.

For example, one host might provide calm continuity, a guest might notice
musicianship or production, and another might bring newer discoveries or a
slightly different generational view. Occasional warm disagreement can make
the programme feel lived in. The key word is *occasional*: do not require a
debate, a fact or a callback in every link.

## Include useful boundaries

The best boundaries prevent a predictable bad habit rather than flattening the
show’s voice. These are strong examples:

- Do not turn the programme into a music lecture.
- Do not treat every link as a fresh start.
- Do not explain every connection between tracks.
- Give listeners company, not advice.
- Leave room for the music when the moment needs it.

They work because they describe an outcome the listener can hear. “Do not be
repetitive” is less useful; it does not tell the DJ what to do instead.

## Keep it short enough to use

One or two focused paragraphs are usually enough. If a brief needs many
exceptions, it may be trying to replace show settings, a playlist, a Soul or a
Musical Leaning. Move that material to the right place, then read the brief
aloud: it should sound like the premise of a programme, not a configuration
file.

## A quick check before saving

Ask yourself:

1. Can I picture the listener’s moment and why this programme belongs there?
2. Is the musical territory in structured settings rather than hidden in prose?
3. Does each host or guest contribute something distinct?
4. Have I named a few useful habits and restraints without scripting every
   break?
5. Would the show still feel recognisable after a week, not just its first
   hour?

If the answer is yes, the DJ has enough direction to sound intentional and
enough room to sound alive.
